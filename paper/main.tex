\begin{abstract}
We introduce \textbf{PhishLens}, a lightweight, explainable URL phishing
detector, built on \textbf{PhishDriftBench} (a four-axis benchmark spanning time,
source, evasion and deployment prevalence) and \textbf{DASH}, a
drift-aware mitigation. Published detectors routinely
report 97--99.5\% accuracy, but an audit of ten recent papers (2024--2026)
finds none evaluates under a temporal split, reports precision at a
realistic deployment base rate, or tests its detector against cataloged
evasion, and only one performs genuine cross-source transfer.
Re-evaluating five architectures under PhishDriftBench, the
reported--deployed gap proves real but uneven: temporal degradation was
undetectable (all five within 0.003 AUC of ceiling); cross-source
transfer cost up to 8.9 AUC points and reordered the leaderboard; rule-based
evasion mostly failed, while generative evasion cost every model 4.5--5.4
recall points uniformly. Sharpest of all is prevalence correction: at a
realistic 1-in-10{,}000 rate, precision for three of five baselines falls
to 4.8--11.2\% despite statistically indistinguishable 98--99\% balanced accuracy: accuracy is shown to be actively uninformative
about deployment false-alarm rate. A provenance audit (285 live lookups) finds
no accuracy gain from lookup features ($-0.51$ AUC), and DASH's null
result on this corpus traces to an easy negative class, not the
mitigation: a harder legitimate class produced real drift alarms at a
cost of 20 labels.

We then build \textbf{PhishLens} (B6), adding one fix per weakness and
testing each in isolation: cleaning alone does not help precision,
adversarial augmentation trades recall for a higher false-positive rate,
and a two-stage cascade (a real classifier, not a heuristic) recovers that
cost and more, cutting false positives sixfold and raising precision
at $\pi{=}10^{-4}$ from 1.8\% to 9.95\%, above three of the five original
baselines. \textbf{B7}'s unsupervised novelty gate shows a naive
version's recall gain collapses precision to 0.20\%, while an orthogonal
revision recovers 75\% of the benefit at a third of the cost. PhishLens
exposes every verdict through exact TreeSHAP attribution, brand-jacking
reason codes and a calibrated abstention band, implemented in a working
tool. Auditing this layer shows 67.15\% of its attribution weight rests
on features independently flagged drift-brittle, and traces a real misclassification (a legitimate Wikipedia URL scored 0.9999
phishing) to exactly that reliance: an explanation faithful today carries no
guarantee of remaining faithful as traffic drifts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Phishing remains among the most economically damaging classes of
cybercrime, and URL-based detection is attractive because it operates
before any content is fetched or rendered, imposing negligible latency
and no rendering infrastructure. A large body of work reports this
problem close to solved: accuracies of 98.29\%~\cite{remya2024resmlp},
99.45\%~\cite{kustiawan2025feateng}, 99.48\%~\cite{kustiawan2025phishofe}
and 99.35\%~\cite{goenka2025layered} have all appeared in the last two
years.

These numbers are not fraudulent, but answer a question that is not the
deployment question. Almost without exception they come from randomly
partitioning a single corpus collected over one time window, balanced at
roughly equal class prevalence, with no adversary in the loop. Each of
those four choices is individually defensible and
collectively misleading:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Random splits leak time.} Phishing URLs arrive in campaigns
      (one kit, one registrar burst, thousands of near-identical strings),
      so a random split grades the model on near-duplicates of what it
      memorised.
\item \textbf{Single-source evaluation hides source-specific artifacts.}
      A feature can carry \emph{opposite} meaning in different corpora:
      Misiek and Hyla~\cite{misiek2026xgboost} report HTTPS presence
      characterising 100\% of legitimate URLs in PhiUSIIL but only 59.2\%
      in their own corpus.
\item \textbf{Balanced test sets hide the base-rate problem.} A 99\%
      accuracy, 1\% false-positive detector deployed at $1{:}10^4$
      prevalence yields precision near 1\%, roughly ninety-nine false alarms per
      true detection. Reported accuracy carries none of this information.
\item \textbf{Adversaries are catalogued but never applied.} Li \emph{et
      al.}~\cite{li2023cloak} systematically review cloaking and evasion
      techniques; no method paper in our corpus evaluates its detector
      against them.
\end{enumerate}

Exactly one paper in our corpus measures the consequence. Misiek and
Hyla~\cite{misiek2026xgboost} report 97.80\% under single-dataset evaluation
and 77.84\% averaged across three sources, a 19.96 percentage-point collapse
that they correctly attribute to dataset shift, ``a factor often overlooked in
existing literature.''

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1.} An evaluation-protocol audit of ten recent phishing
      detection papers, eight of them method studies from 2024--2026
      (Table~\ref{tab:audit}), establishing that the
      protocol gap is current rather than historical.
\item \textbf{C2.} \textbf{PhishDriftBench}: a four-axis protocol evaluating
      detectors across time (Axis~T), across sources (Axis~S), against
      rule-based and generative evasion (Axis~E), and at deployment-realistic
      class priors.
\item \textbf{C3.} A \textbf{feature-provenance and lookup-dependency audit}
      that partitions features by whether they require a network lookup, and
      quantifies the accuracy attributable to retrospective-lookup artifacts.
\item \textbf{C4.} A \textbf{drift-stability scoring} method that identifies
      drift-brittle features before deployment.
\item \textbf{C5.} \textbf{DASH}, a lightweight drift-aware mitigation combining
      stability-weighted features, unsupervised drift detection, a bounded
      active-labelling budget and a calibrated abstention band.
\item \textbf{C6.} \textbf{B6}, a model built incrementally so each
      component's contribution is measured, not assumed: data cleaning,
      stability-weighted training, adversarial generative augmentation,
      and a two-stage cascade consulted only where Stage~1 is not
      confident.
\item \textbf{C7.} \textbf{B7}, an unsupervised novelty gate for zero-day
      phishing: a naive version's recall benefit does not survive contact
      with prevalence-corrected precision, but diagnosing why motivates a
      revision (an orthogonal, character-sequence signal) that recovers
      most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost.
\item \textbf{C8.} \textbf{PhishLens}: we name the deployed detector
      (B6's v3 cascade) and add an explainability layer (exact TreeSHAP
      attribution, brand-jacking reason codes, a calibrated
      \textsc{uncertain} verdict) implemented in a working tool.
      Auditing it against C4's scores shows 67.15\% of its attribution
      weight rests on features independently flagged drift-brittle, and we
      trace a real misclassification (a legitimate Wikipedia URL scored
      0.9999) to exactly that reliance.
\end{itemize}

Throughout, comparative claims are backed by nonparametric bootstrap 95\%
confidence intervals over the relevant test set (1{,}000 resamples),
added in this revision so headline numbers carry an explicit uncertainty
estimate rather than a bare point value (Tables~\ref{tab:axist},
\ref{tab:axiss}, \ref{tab:prevalence}).

\section{Related Work and Protocol Audit}

\subsection{Feature-engineering and classical ML approaches}

Kustiawan and Ghauth compare URL-, HTML- and derived-feature sets across
ten classifiers~\cite{kustiawan2025feateng} and later propose
PhishOFE~\cite{kustiawan2025phishofe}, an optimised feature-engineering
framework avoiding third-party features, reaching 99.48\% with CatBoost.
Goenka \emph{et al.}~\cite{goenka2025layered} propose a two-layer model
where a domain-squatting/obfuscation screen precedes a lexical classifier
(99.35\% at 12.49\,ms), but only 89.17\% on an older corpus, which they
attribute, correctly, to 2025's top targeted brands differing from 2018's:
a direct observation of concept drift, measured but untreated.

\subsection{Deep and hybrid architectures}

Remya \emph{et al.}~\cite{remya2024resmlp} apply a residual pipeline of
convolutional and inverted-residual blocks feeding an MLP over lexical,
sentiment and domain-age features, later proposing
BGL-PhishNet~\cite{remya2025bgl} (BERT + GNN + LightGBM, 97.3\% on
549{,}346 URLs). He \emph{et al.}~\cite{he2024featext} introduce a
TextRank feature-extension library feeding a BERT--CNN classifier.
Vulfin \emph{et al.}~\cite{vulfin2026multimodal} fuse four modalities
(CatBoost, a character CNN, CodeBERT, EfficientNet-B7 over screenshots)
with SHAP/Grad-CAM explanation, and are among the few to quantify
temporal decay (8--15\% degradation).

\subsection{AI-generated phishing URLs, and reviews of the field}

Early work assumed a narrow generative model:
DeepPhish~\cite{bahnsen2018deepphish}, a recurrent network fitted to one
threat actor's URLs, was for years the only documented URL generator, and
detectors were evaluated against it accordingly, a premise that does not
hold in 2026, when general-purpose language models produce diverse
candidate URLs at negligible cost. \textbf{Whether a detector validated
against a narrow, historical generator retains its recall against a
contemporary one is, to our knowledge, untested for URL-only detection.}
Axis~E2 tests this directly. Li \emph{et al.}~\cite{li2023cloak}
systematically review server- and client-side cloaking, documenting how
phishing infrastructure fingerprints requesters to serve benign content
selectively to analysis systems; Kavya and
Sumathi~\cite{kavya2025review} survey the field from list-based through
deep and graph-based methods, naming dataset diversity, adversarial
robustness and interpretability as unfilled gaps.

\section{Threat Model and Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Deployment setting}

We consider a detector $f_\theta : \mathcal{U} \rightarrow [0,1]$ mapping a URL
string $u \in \mathcal{U}$ to a phishing score, deployed inline in a browser
extension or mail gateway. Three properties distinguish deployment from the
standard experimental setting:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Temporal ordering.} The detector is trained on URLs observed up to
      time $T$ and must classify URLs first observed after $T$. The joint
      distribution $P_t(u, y)$ is non-stationary.
\item \textbf{Severe class imbalance.} Deployment prevalence
      $\pi = P(y{=}1)$ lies in the range $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-4}$, not $0.5$.
\item \textbf{Adaptive adversary.} The attacker observes deployed detectors and
      applies label-preserving transformations $\tau$ such that $\tau(u)$ remains
      an effective phishing URL while $f_\theta(\tau(u))$ falls below threshold.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Adversary capabilities}

We assume the adversary can (i) apply lexical transformations to a URL without
altering its function, (ii) generate novel URL strings using a contemporary
generative model, and (iii) control any \emph{lookup channel} the detector depends on, for example
serving a benign redirect target to a detector that
expands shortened URLs, consistent with the cloaking behaviour documented
in~\cite{li2023cloak}. We do \emph{not} assume white-box gradient access to the
deployed model.

\subsection{The reported--deployed gap}

Let $A_{\text{rand}}$ denote accuracy under a random split of a single corpus,
and $A_{\text{deploy}}$ denote accuracy under temporal, cross-source and
adversarial conditions at deployment prevalence. We define the
\emph{reported--deployed gap}
\begin{equation}
\Delta = A_{\text{rand}} - A_{\text{deploy}},
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
and take the estimation of $\Delta$, its decomposition into temporal,
cross-source, provenance and adversarial components, and its mitigation, as the
object of this work.

\section{PhishDriftBench}

\subsection{Axis T: temporal generalisation}
\label{sec:axist-caveat}

Given a corpus with first-observation timestamps, we select a cut point $T$,
train on $\{u : t(u) \le T\}$, and evaluate on disjoint windows
$(T, T{+}1]$, $(T, T{+}3]$, $(T, T{+}6]$ and $(T, T{+}12]$ months. The output
is a \emph{decay curve} $A(\delta)$ rather than a scalar. Where a source lacks
per-URL timestamps we fall back to dated dataset \emph{releases} as a coarse
time axis, exploiting the 2018--2026 span of the corpora in this study.

\paragraph{Asymmetric timestamp granularity (methodological caveat).} Only
PhishTank carries genuine per-URL timestamps among the acquired corpora;
PhiUSIIL and Tranco carry a single dated-release/snapshot stamp and
cannot be temporally split. Table~\ref{tab:axist} therefore varies only
the phishing side by real date, holding the legitimate side to a single fixed Tranco sample. This isolates
decay in \emph{recognising new phishing} under a stationary-negative-class
assumption, not joint drift in the legitimate population. Separately, PhishTank's bulk file is
survivorship-filtered to URLs verified \emph{currently} online, not a
stable historical archive. Both caveats are revisited in Limitations.

\subsection{Axis S: cross-source generalisation}

For sources $S_1, \dots, S_n$ we evaluate all ordered pairs
$(S_i \rightarrow S_j)$, $i \neq j$, plus leave-one-source-out. Crucially,
models are trained \emph{once} and transferred, in contrast
to~\cite{goenka2025layered}, which retrains per dataset.

\subsection{Axis E: evasion}

\paragraph{E1, rule-based} We apply label-preserving transformations drawn from
the cloaking and obfuscation taxonomy of~\cite{li2023cloak}: homoglyph and IDN
substitution, subdomain padding, path padding, percent-encoding, shortener
wrapping, TLD substitution and hyphenated brand insertion. We report recall
degradation per transformation.

\paragraph{E2, generative} We evaluate against URL strings produced by a
contemporary generative model, and compare directly against the
DeepPhish-era~\cite{bahnsen2018deepphish} condition under which early
AI-generated-URL detection claims were validated. The research question is
stated precisely: \emph{does a detector validated against a 2018-generation URL
generator retain its recall against a 2024+ generation generator?}

\subsection{Prevalence correction}

For each operating point we report precision, false-positive rate and expected
alerts per $10^6$ URLs at $\pi \in \{10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}\}$. Given a
threshold $\tau$ with true-positive rate $\mathrm{TPR}(\tau)$ and false-positive
rate $\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)$ measured on a balanced test set, precision at
prevalence $\pi$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Prec}_\pi(\tau) =
\frac{\pi \cdot \mathrm{TPR}(\tau)}
     {\pi \cdot \mathrm{TPR}(\tau) + (1-\pi) \cdot \mathrm{FPR}(\tau)} .
\label{eq:prec}
\end{equation}

\section{Feature-Provenance and Lookup-Dependency Audit}
\label{sec:prov}

\subsection{Provenance taxonomy}
\label{sec:prov-classes}

We classify every feature appearing in the surveyed method papers into three
provenance classes:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{P1, static-lexical.} Computable from the URL string alone, with no
      network access: length, token counts, entropy, character-class ratios,
      TLD identity, presence of suspicious tokens.
\item \textbf{P2, lookup-at-inference.} Requiring a live network operation:
      WHOIS registration date and expiry, DNS records, SSL certificate presence,
      domain age, hosting country, and shortened-URL expansion.
\item \textbf{P3, third-party reputation.} Requiring an external ranking or
      index: Alexa/Tranco rank, PageRank, search-engine indexing status.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The retrospective-lookup artifact}

P2 features carry a failure mode not, to our knowledge, named in this
literature. When a historical corpus is annotated with P2 features by
performing lookups \emph{at write time} rather than at collection time,
the resulting values encode domain \emph{mortality} rather than phishing
semantics: phishing domains are typically taken down within days,
legitimate domains persist for years, so a retrospective query fails for
most phishing entries and succeeds for most legitimate ones, and any
classifier can exploit this without learning anything about phishing. The
hazard is concrete: BGL-PhishNet~\cite{remya2025bgl} reports WHOIS/DNS/SSL
and host-based features over a 549{,}346-URL corpus, summarised by a
single aggregate ``average domain age'' with no per-class breakdown. We
therefore measure, rather than assume, the magnitude of this effect.

\subsection{Audit experiments}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Ablation.} Retrain each baseline with P2 and P3 features removed;
      report $\Delta$accuracy.
\item \textbf{Provenance-timing contrast.} Where a corpus permits it, compare
      P2 features resolved at collection time against the same features resolved
      today; report the accuracy difference and the per-class lookup-failure
      rate.
\item \textbf{Lookup-channel adversary.} Measure recall when the lookup channel
      is (a) unavailable and (b) adversarially controlled, per the cloaking
      model of~\cite{li2023cloak}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Duplicate and near-duplicate leakage probe}

Independently of provenance, we quantify exact and near-duplicate URLs
straddling the train/test boundary in every corpus used, since campaign
structure makes such duplicates likely under random and $k$-fold partitioning.
We report duplicate rate and accuracy before and after deduplication for each
dataset, and recommend that this figure be reported as standard.

\section{Drift-Stability Feature Scoring}
\label{sec:stability-method}

For feature $j$ we combine distributional stability across time windows and
across sources with the variance of its permutation importance. Let
$\mathrm{PSI}_t(j)$ and $\mathrm{PSI}_s(j)$ denote the Population Stability
Index of feature $j$ computed across consecutive temporal windows and across
source pairs respectively, and let $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{imp}}(j)$ denote the
variance of its permutation importance across those partitions. We define the
stability score
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Stab}(j) = \Big[\alpha\,\mathrm{PSI}_t(j)
                      + \beta\,\mathrm{PSI}_s(j)
                      + \gamma\,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{imp}}(j)\Big]^{-1},
\label{eq:stab}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ fixed on a validation split and never on test data.
Features are partitioned into \emph{drift-stable} and \emph{drift-brittle} at a
threshold selected on validation data only.

A concrete validation target exists: the HTTPS inversion between PhiUSIIL and
the custom corpus of~\cite{misiek2026xgboost} should be flagged as drift-brittle
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:stab} \emph{without} access to the second corpus's labels.
Whether it is flagged is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{DASH: Drift-Aware Selective Hardening}
\label{sec:dash-method}

DASH is deliberately lightweight, so that it remains deployable at the latency
budget URL-only detection exists to satisfy. It has four components:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Stability-weighted training.} Train on features weighted by
      $\mathrm{Stab}(j)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:stab}, down-weighting drift-brittle
      features.
\item \textbf{Unsupervised drift detection.} Run ADWIN (or DDM) over the stream
      of prediction confidences. This requires \emph{no labels}, which is the
      binding constraint in deployment.
\item \textbf{Bounded active update.} On a drift alarm, select the most
      uncertain $b\%$ of the current window ($b \approx 2$), obtain labels for
      that subset only, and warm-restart or partially refit.
\item \textbf{Calibrated abstention.} Define a confidence band in which the
      model returns \textsc{uncertain} and escalates to a heavier check rather
      than guessing. We report a coverage--risk curve rather than a single
      accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

The claim under test is that DASH recovers a substantial fraction of temporal
and cross-source degradation at a small fraction of the labelling cost of full
retraining. It is stated as a hypothesis and evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

Sections~\ref{sec:results} and \ref{sec:discussion} establish four concrete
weaknesses in B1--B5. This section proposes one model, built incrementally,
that targets each weakness directly rather than proposing architecture
changes disconnected from what was actually measured. Each version below
adds exactly one component on top of the last, so that its individual
contribution can be isolated experimentally (Sec.~\ref{sec:b6-results}).

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{v0 (clean data).} Targets two quantified
data artifacts: 19.4\% near-duplication in PhishTank
(Table~\ref{tab:dupe}) and homoglyph substitution's unpunished effect on
B3 (Table~\ref{tab:axise1}). v0 applies LSH near-duplicate removal and
Unicode confusable normalization (mapping each non-ASCII character to its
true-Latin visual confusable) as a \emph{mandatory} preprocessing step
before training an otherwise-unchanged gradient-boosted lexical
classifier.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{v1 (+ stability-weighted training).}
Features are weighted by $\mathrm{Stab}(j)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:stab}) via
XGBoost's native \texttt{feature\_weights}, biasing column subsampling
away from drift-brittle features (Sec.~\ref{sec:stability-method}). This
is DASH's first component (Sec.~VI), applied here as a default training
recipe rather than only inside the DASH loop.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{v2 (+ adversarial generative augmentation).}
The training set is augmented with 3{,}000 synthetic URLs (1{,}500 each)
from the two character-Markov generators used for Axis~E2, labelled phishing. This targets the one finding that hit
every baseline uniformly:
recall loss against a contemporary-style generator
(Table~\ref{tab:axise2}).

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{v3 (+ two-stage cascade).} v2 becomes a
high-recall Stage~1 filter: URLs scoring below $0.1$ are dispositioned
negative immediately; the rest pass to Stage~2, a real B5 (BERT
embeddings + LightGBM) fit once on the same training set, whose score
becomes final for routed URLs. This targets the largest effect measured
in this work: precision collapse at deployment prevalence
(Table~\ref{tab:prevalence}). An initial Stage~2 used a hand-rolled
nearest-BERT-centroid heuristic instead of a trained classifier; it
collapsed recall from $0.99$ to $0.757$ with no precision gain. It is
reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:b6-results} as evidence about cascade
\emph{design} (Stage~2 must be an actually-trained classifier), not
against the cascade \emph{idea}, which is why v3 uses the real,
already-validated B5 pipeline instead.
\smallskip

\subsection{PhishLens: naming the deployed system, and its explainability layer}
\label{sec:phishlens}

We call the resulting detector \textbf{PhishLens}: the v3 cascade above
(Stage~1: 33 P1 lexical features + XGBoost; Stage~2: DistilBERT embeddings
+ LightGBM for the subset Stage~1 does not confidently clear),
instrumented with three additions that make its decisions inspectable
rather than opaque, none of which changes the classification numbers in
Sec.~\ref{sec:b6-results}, since they sit downstream of the score, not
inside it.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Per-URL feature attribution.} Every score carries an \emph{exact} TreeSHAP explanation, computed with
XGBoost's native \texttt{pred\_contribs=True} for Stage~1 (the same values
the \texttt{shap} package's \texttt{TreeExplainer} would produce, with no
approximation), reporting which features pushed the score toward
      \textsc{phishing} or \textsc{legitimate}, and by how much.
\item \textbf{Brand-jacking reason codes.} B2's domain-squatting screen
      (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}) is exposed as a human-readable reason
      (e.g.\ ``looks like a misspelling of a known brand domain'') rather
      than folded silently into the score.
\item \textbf{Calibrated abstention.} DASH's abstention band
      (Sec.~\ref{sec:dash-method}, component 4) is surfaced as a third
      verdict, \textsc{uncertain}, with a stated reason (score inside the
      calibrated band, or a link-shortener whose destination this system deliberately does not
      resolve). It refuses to guess past the point the model has evidence
      for, rather than forcing a binary answer.
\end{enumerate}

All three are implemented in a working tool (CLI and web), not only
described. The quantitative results in
Sec.~\ref{sec:explainability-results} use a 33-feature XGBoost classifier
matching Stage~1, fit independently on a similarly-constructed sample
(weights uniform for this fit, not Table~\ref{tab:b6}'s exact weights);
the claim under test is the ranking of feature importance, not one
fitted model's precise score.

\subsection{B7: an unsupervised novelty gate for zero-day phishing}
\label{sec:b7-method}

B1--B6 share a structural ceiling no amount of retraining removes: every
one is purely supervised, recognising only phishing \emph{patterns present in its training data},
the direct cause of Axis~E2's 4.5--5.4
recall-point loss against an unseen contemporary generator
(Table~\ref{tab:axise2}). B7 proposes a structurally different mechanism:
an Isolation Forest~\cite{liu2008isolation}-style anomaly detector fit
\emph{only} on legitimate URLs, flagging anything far from the
``normal-legitimate'' manifold regardless of the supervised cascade's verdict, the one case a purely
supervised model cannot handle by construction.

Three combination rules are tested against B6's v2+cascade as the
supervised component:
\smallskip\noindent\textbf{OR-gate.} Flag phishing if the cascade says so
\emph{or} the novelty score exceeds a threshold calibrated on the 95th
percentile of a legitimate \emph{validation} split.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Gated refinement.} Let novelty escalate only
URLs the cascade is already uncertain about (score $\in [0.15, 0.5)$),
rather than blanket-flagging every novel-looking URL.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{AND-gate (revision).} Both above operate on one novelty signal, the Isolation Forest on the same
33 structural features the supervised cascade uses. Sec.~\ref{sec:b7-results} traces
the OR-gate's false-positive cost to exactly this overlap: ``structurally
unusual'' and ``generated-style phishing text'' share a large fraction of
ordinary legitimate URLs. The AND-gate adds a second signal on a genuinely different feature space, namely
character \emph{sequences}: an
order-4 character Markov model (add-$k$-smoothed, $k=0.5$) fit only on
legitimate URL text, scoring average per-character surprisal. A
structurally-unusual but textually-ordinary legitimate URL should score
low here even where the structural gate scores it high; Axis~E2's
Markov-generated text is fit on \emph{phishing} strings, so it should
score high on both signals. Escalation requires \emph{both} to agree (same calibration), which is
strictly more conservative than the OR-gate by construction, since it can only suppress false positives the OR-gate
would raise, never add new ones.
\smallskip

Axis~E2's contemporary-generator URLs serve as the zero-day proxy: they
are, by construction, phishing text no component of B6 has trained on.

\subsection{Baselines}

We re-implement five architectures spanning the design space of the surveyed
corpus:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{B1: Gradient-boosted lexical.} CatBoost/XGBoost over
      engineered lexical features, following~\cite{kustiawan2025feateng,
      kustiawan2025phishofe}.
\item \textbf{B2: Layered brand-jacking.} Domain-squatting and obfuscation
      screen followed by lexical classification,
      following~\cite{goenka2025layered}.
\item \textbf{B3: Residual MLP.} Convolutional and inverted-residual blocks
      feeding an MLP, following~\cite{remya2024resmlp}.
\item \textbf{B4: Cross-dataset gradient boosting.} XGBoost over the
      cross-dataset feature intersection of~\cite{misiek2026xgboost}. This is the
      strongest published transfer baseline in our corpus and therefore the
      reference our cross-source axis must improve upon.
\item \textbf{B5: Transformer--GBM hybrid.} BERT embeddings fused with
      LightGBM, following~\cite{remya2025bgl}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Reproduction notes.} (i) The graph component
of~\cite{remya2025bgl} is specified only as capturing ``relationships
between query parameters and domains,'' not in enough detail to
reproduce; we evaluate B5 as BERT+LightGBM only. (ii) B4 follows
\cite{misiek2026xgboost}'s feature-intersection procedure, recomputed per
experiment rather than reusing the published set where the intersection
depends on which corpora are paired.

\subsection{Metrics}

Balanced accuracy, precision, recall and $F_1$ are reported for comparability
with prior work. Primary reporting is ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, and
prevalence-corrected precision by Eq.~\eqref{eq:prec} at
$\pi \in \{10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\}$. For DASH we additionally report
coverage--risk curves and labels consumed.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

All experiments use URL strings only; no page is fetched, rendered or hosted at
any point. Hardware: Apple Silicon (arm64), 12 CPU cores, 24\,GB RAM, no
GPU. Software: Python 3.14, scikit-learn 1.9, XGBoost 3.3, LightGBM 4.7,
CatBoost 1.2, PyTorch 2.13 (CPU-only), Transformers 5.14.

\textbf{Data and code availability.} The implementation (feature
extraction, provenance taxonomy, split engine, baselines B1--B5, B6/B7,
DASH), evaluation scripts, a 78-test unit suite, and the exact benchmark
splits used here will be released via a public repository upon

decisions. Raw datasets are not redistributed, since each is publicly available from
its original source (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}) under that

The result is, on this corpus, a near-total negative: of the seven E1
transforms, six leave recall essentially unchanged or \emph{increase} it.
Path padding and shortener wrapping push recall to 1.0000 for four of
five models, because the transform adds exactly the signal these lexical

uniformly robust of the five across every axis tested, despite being
competitive or best in-distribution.

The balanced-accuracy figures this literature reports (all $\ge 0.98$

$\pi{=}10^{-2}$, already optimistic for most deployments, three of five
baselines fall below 90\% precision. At $\pi{=}10^{-4}$, B4 reaches \textbf{4.8\%} precision, roughly twenty false alerts per true

than any TPR advantage, since TPR is statistically indistinguishable
across all five models (98.8--99.2\%). \textbf{This is the sharpest
illustration in this work of the reported--deployed gap}: accuracy, the
number every surveyed paper leads with, is actively uninformative about
which model would generate fewer false alarms in a real deployment.

\subsection{Feature provenance}
\label{sec:provenance-results}

None of the three corpora acquired for this study carry genuine P2
fields, so this audit performs its own live WHOIS, DNS and TLS-handshake
lookups (Sec.~\ref{sec:prov-classes}) against 285 real domains: 200
Tranco legitimate domains and 85 unique PhishTank phishing domains, split
30 ``old'' (submitted $>1$ year before the historical cut used elsewhere
in this paper) and 55 ``recent'' (submitted within the last 30 days).

Three results, and none confirms the naive domain-mortality hypothesis.
\textbf{(1) Ablation.} Adding all P2 features to the P1 lexical set does not improve accuracy: AUC \emph{falls} from 0.9840 (P1-only) to
0.9788
(P1+P2), $n=86$; the lexical signal is already strong enough that noisy,
partially-missing lookup features slightly hurt rather than help.
\textbf{(2) Lookup-signal-alone.} The three binary success flags with no
lexical features reach AUC \textbf{0.5635}, barely above chance.
\textbf{(3) The class-conditional breakdown does not point where
expected.} Old phishing submissions show \emph{higher} DNS/SSL success
(86.7\%/83.3\%) than recent ones (58.2\%/47.3\%), the reverse of what domain mortality
predicts. This is Sec.~\ref{sec:axist-caveat}'s
survivorship caveat operating exactly as described: PhishTank's bulk file
only contains URLs verified \emph{currently online}, so a surviving
``old'' submission is by construction one that did not die, while
``recent'' submissions are caught earlier in a lifecycle that, for many,
ends in exactly the DNS/SSL failure the mortality hypothesis predicts.
This is a selection effect from PhishTank's own filtering, not domain
mortality directly.

\textbf{Caveat.} Rate-limited live lookups triggered occasional
connection and timeout failures across all three classes, so some
fraction of every rate in Table~\ref{tab:provenance} is
lookup-infrastructure noise rather than a true domain-status signal; at
$n=285$ this cannot be separated from genuine failure without a larger,
retried sweep. The honest summary is a negative result on this sample (P2 features demonstrate none of the
inflation this audit was designed to detect) alongside a real,
mechanistically-explained
survivorship reversal that a larger, non-survivorship-filtered sweep
would be needed to confirm generalises.

Fit on a 16{,}000-URL validation split spanning both sources, real dates
on the phishing side, Eq.~\eqref{eq:stab} partitions the 33 P1 features
into 23 drift-stable and 10 drift-brittle at the 30th-percentile
threshold. The validation target proposed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:stability-method} is \textbf{not} reproduced here:
\texttt{has\_https} scores $\mathrm{Stab}=186.0$, confidently
drift-\emph{stable}, not the inversion X-PHIDE~\cite{misiek2026xgboost}
found on a different corpus pairing (PhiUSIIL vs.\ their own custom
corpus and GramBeddings, neither part of this study). This means the
specific validation target does not transfer across corpus choice, itself informative about how
corpus-specific drift-brittleness is.

The ten features flagged brittle (\texttt{url\_length},
\texttt{hostname\_length}, \texttt{path\_length}, \texttt{num\_dots},
\texttt{num\_slashes}, \texttt{num\_digits}, \texttt{num\_subdomains},

brand/token feature. This independently confirms a bug found by
inspection in this project's own auxiliary demo tool (outside the
paper's protocol): it initially misclassified every legitimate URL
carrying a path, because both acquired legitimate-URL sources contain
\emph{zero} URLs with a path beyond the bare domain while 94\% of
PhishTank's phishing URLs do. Every feature that bug touches is exactly
the set Eq.~\eqref{eq:stab} independently flags brittle, with no
knowledge of the incident and no access to target-corpus labels. The formal score and an unrelated,
manually-debugged failure converging on
the same features is stronger evidence than either finding alone.

On the original 12-month stream, DASH matches no adaptation for a
legible reason: its unsupervised ADWIN detector recorded \textbf{zero

finding: PhishTank-vs-Tranco is too separable to
exhibit measurable temporal degradation, so there is no drift signal to
catch (full retrain reaches only 0.9999, barely above 0.9952).
\textbf{DASH cannot be credited with, or faulted for, recovering
degradation Axis~T did not detect.}

\paragraph{Stress test 1: widest real temporal gap.} Rebuilding the
protocol at the most extreme gap PhishTank's timestamps support (train
through 2023, stream all 2026) still gives zero alarms, no-adaptation within 0.0043 AUC of the retrain ceiling, ruling out ``the

window width.

\paragraph{Stress test 2: a harder negative class.} Substituting PhiUSIIL
(a harder negative per Axis~S) for Tranco, everything else held constant,
gives this paper's first genuine prospective validation of DASH's drift
detector: ADWIN recorded \textbf{2 real drift alarms} and 2 warm-restarts,
spending only 20 labels (0.1\% of the stream) versus 20{,}000 for a full
retrain, at AUC (0.9990) statistically indistinguishable from no-adaptation. The detector fires
when the task has drift headroom, at
a fraction of full-retrain cost. Together, the three columns isolate the
cause precisely: it was the negative class's low difficulty, not window
width, that suppressed drift detection. The coverage--risk curve is left
for future work alongside a labelled stream long enough to exercise the
abstention band's calibration.

The naive OR-gate genuinely improves zero-day-proxy recall (+5.60 points)
but does so by flagging 4.84\% of \emph{real legitimate} URLs as novel, roughly $12\times$ the cascade alone's 0.04\% FPR, which
Eq.~\eqref{eq:prec} converts into a precision collapse at $\pi=10^{-4}$
from 18.10\% to \textbf{0.20\%}. The gated refinement, escalating only
URLs the cascade is already uncertain about, avoids that cost (FPR rises
only to 0.09\%, precision recovers to 9.95\%) but at the price of the
benefit: recall gain falls to +0.50 points, barely above noise at
$n{=}1{,}000$.

\textbf{Revision: a second signal from a different feature space.} The
naive gate's failure traced to a fixable overlap: its Isolation Forest
ran on the same 33 structural features the supervised cascade already
uses, so ``structurally unusual'' caught ordinary-but-diverse legitimate
URLs (long paths, many hyphens, deep subdomains) along with genuinely
novel phishing style. The AND-gate instead adds a second, independently-fit signal (a character-level
language-surprisal model fit only on legitimate URL \emph{text}) and
requires both to agree.
The result (Table~\ref{tab:b7}) is a better operating point: FPR of
1.61\% (a $3\times$ reduction) while retaining +4.20 of the naive gate's +5.60 recall points, 75\% of the
benefit at roughly a third of the cost, though precision (0.61\%) is still far below the cascade alone
(18.10\%).

The revised reading narrows rather than reverses the original diagnosis:
no gate built from a \emph{single} signal correlated with the supervised
stage's own features escapes this trade-off cheaply, but a signal from an
orthogonal representation changes its shape substantially without eliminating it, and this is
visible only by testing against the metric that matters, not the recall
number alone.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Does the random-split leaderboard survive under this protocol?}

Not testably on Axis~T: every baseline sat within 0.003 AUC of ceiling at
every horizon, leaving no leaderboard spread to reorder
(Table~\ref{tab:axist}, $\tau=-0.20$, $p=0.82$, uninformative at $n=5$).
Axis~S had headroom, and there the in-distribution ranking (B5 $>$ B3
$\approx$ B2 $\approx$ B1 $\approx$ B4, all within 0.2 AUC points) is
\emph{not} the cross-source ranking: B3 moves from second-best
in-distribution to worst-in-one-direction under transfer
(Table~\ref{tab:axiss}), and is independently singled out again by Axis~E1's evasion table, with two different
mechanisms (transfer direction, homoglyph sensitivity) pointing at the
same baseline. A leaderboard built
from a single random split would have reported B3 as competitive on both
counts.

\subsection{How does the reported--deployed gap $\Delta$ decompose here?}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\columnwidth]{fig3_where_gap_shows_up.png}
\caption{Where the reported--deployed gap actually surfaced, per axis.
Axis~T, purpose-built to expose temporal decay, found essentially none,
because the task sat at ceiling. The other three axes each found
real, independently-caused degradation.}
\label{fig:gap}
\end{figure}

Prevalence dominates by a wide margin. Cross-source transfer costs at
most 8.9 AUC points (Table~\ref{tab:axiss}); evasion costs at most 2.75
recall points for one baseline under one transform
(Table~\ref{tab:axise1}); the temporal component could not be measured
above noise. Prevalence correction turns 98--99\% accuracy into
4.8--20.2\% precision for four of five baselines at $\pi=10^{-4}$
(Table~\ref{tab:prevalence}), an order of magnitude larger than the other
three components combined, obtained without retraining anything or
crossing a dataset boundary. The provenance component (C3, 285 live
lookups) contributes nothing in the predicted direction ($-0.51$ AUC from
adding P2 features) and does not reorder this decomposition.

\subsection{Do contemporary generative models defeat 2018-era-validated detectors?}

Narrowly yes. Axis~E2 (Table~\ref{tab:axise2}) tests two order-4
character-Markov generators fit on the same corpus's older and newer
submissions, not DeepPhish or an independent contemporary LLM
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}). Every baseline loses 4.5--5.4 recall points
moving to the contemporary generator, including B5, which showed no comparable vulnerability on Axis~S or Axis~E1. The effect is consistent
in direction and magnitude across four architecturally distinct baselines
and appears only when the generator's \emph{training era} changes,
evidence recent phishing style has drifted beyond current detectors. Axis~E1 supplies the
contrast: hand-crafted perturbations mostly fail and twice backfire,
where a learned generator succeeds.

\subsection{Does stability scoring predict brittleness prospectively, or only explain it in hindsight?}

Prospectively, at least once. Eq.~\eqref{eq:stab} was fit with no access
to the interactive demo's failure (a separate tool), yet flagged exactly
the ten length/count features responsible for it as drift-brittle (Sec.~\ref{sec:stability-method}), evidence the score is
not merely re-describing a known-bad feature after the fact. Its
literature-motivated target, flagging \texttt{has\_https} the way
X-PHIDE's corpora would demand, did \emph{not} reproduce here: the score
generalised to a failure mode it was never tuned for, but not to a named
target measured on different corpora.

\subsection{Is an explainable detector's explanation itself trustworthy?}

Not unconditionally. PhishLens's TreeSHAP attributions are faithful to
what the model computed, but that is not reliability of the model's
reasoning about a given URL. Because 67.15\% of its attribution weight
concentrates in the same ten features flagged drift-brittle, explanation
and prediction share a common failure mode rather than one checking the
other: \texttt{en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phishing} shows SHAP faithfully
reporting \emph{why} the model got it wrong, without signalling
\emph{that} it did. This is why we pair the explainability layer with the
calibrated abstention band (Sec.~\ref{sec:phishlens}) rather than treat
either alone as sufficient: abstention is calibrated on the score
distribution, not the attributions the prediction rests on.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

This work is defensive. All experiments operate on URL \emph{strings}
within offline datasets: no phishing page is authored, hosted, served or
transmitted, no live site is contacted for the evasion axes, and no human
subject is involved.

Axis~E2 requires generating candidate phishing URL strings, under three
constraints: generated strings are used solely as classifier inputs and
never registered, resolved or deployed; the generation procedure is
described at the level of feature and distributional statistics
sufficient for reproduction, not released as a packaged generator; and we
release the benchmark splits, evaluation harness and trained detectors (the defensive artifacts) but
deliberately not generator weights or a
turnkey generation pipeline. The underlying capability is already public
(contemporary LLMs generate plausible phishing URLs with no specialised
tooling); this work's contribution is measuring detector resilience to
that capability, information that favours defenders.

%==============================================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Timestamp availability.} Axis~T requires first-observation
      timestamps; dated releases give coarse resolution confounded with
      collection-methodology changes.
\item \textbf{Reproduction fidelity.} Baselines are re-implementations
      from published descriptions. Where incomplete, notably the graph construction in~\cite{remya2025bgl},
      we report our interpretation and do not attribute results to the original
      authors.
\item \textbf{Generator representativeness.} Axis~E2 evaluates one
      contemporary generator, not the space of possible generators; it
      tests whether a published claim generalises, not a worst case.
\item \textbf{URL-only scope.} HTML, screenshot and network-level
      signals are excluded by design; conclusions apply to URL-only
      detection.
\item \textbf{Prevalence estimates.} Estimated from published telemetry,
      not a live deployment.
\item \textbf{Corpus diversity.} Three sources were acquired (PhishTank,
      PhiUSIIL, Tranco); GramBeddings and Kaggle are out of scope by
      project decision (personal identifying information required).
      Axis~S's LOSO column is numerically identical to its off-diagonal
      cell; every cross-source finding is a claim about this pair, not a
      field-wide generalisation.
\item \textbf{Single-seed training.} Every baseline and B6/B7 version is
      fit once (seed 0); bootstrap CIs quantify resampling variance, not training-seed variance, so a narrow CI could still move under a
      different seed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We audited ten recent phishing URL detection papers and found a uniform evaluation protocol (random split, single source, balanced
classes, no adversary) that answers a different question from the deployment
question. We introduced PhishDriftBench (temporal, cross-source, evasion
and prevalence-corrected evaluation), a feature-provenance audit, and
DASH, a lightweight drift-aware mitigation.

The reported--deployed gap proved real but unevenly distributed.
Temporal degradation was undetectable (all five baselines within 0.003
AUC of ceiling); cross-source transfer cost up to 8.9 AUC points and
reordered the leaderboard, worst for the residual-MLP baseline whose
transfer cost inverts with direction; rule-based evasion mostly failed to
reduce recall and backfired for two of seven transforms, while generative
evasion cost every baseline 4.5--5.4 recall points uniformly. Prevalence
correction alone was the largest effect measured: 98--99\% balanced
accuracy became 4.8--20.2\% precision for four of five baselines at
$\pi{=}10^{-4}$, a pattern confirmed by bootstrap CIs despite individual
point-estimate uncertainty. DASH could not be shown to recover
degradation Axis~T itself did not detect on this corpus; stress-testing
traced this to the negative class's difficulty rather than window width or a mitigation defect: substituting a harder
legitimate class produced
the project's first real drift alarms at a cost of 20 labels. The
provenance audit (285 live lookups) found no accuracy gain from lookup
features ($-0.51$ AUC) and near-chance signal from lookup-success flags
alone (0.56 AUC), alongside a survivorship-induced reversal traced to the
source corpus's own filtering rather than domain age.

We then built \textbf{PhishLens} (B6), adding one fix per measured
weakness and testing each in isolation. Adversarial generative
augmentation nearly halved the Axis~E2 recall gap but increased the
false-positive rate; a cascade's second stage had to be an actually-trained classifier (a centroid heuristic collapsed
recall to 0.757 for no gain) before it recovered recall, cut false positives
sixfold, and raised precision at $\pi{=}10^{-4}$ from 1.8\% to 9.95\%,
above three of the five original baselines. This came at a real,
previously-untested cost: 6.6 recall points against Axis~E2's
contemporary generator relative to Stage~1 alone, because Stage~2
inherits the same generative-evasion vulnerability every baseline showed.
B7's unsupervised novelty gate showed the same shape of trade-off: a
naive version recovers $+5.6$ zero-day-proxy recall points but collapses
precision from 18.10\% to 0.20\%; a revision using an orthogonal,
character-sequence signal recovers 75\% of that benefit at a third of the
false-positive cost. Measuring rather than asserting ``lightweight,''
Stage~1 alone is sub-millisecond and sub-megabyte, but cascade throughput
is regime-dependent: 573~URLs/s on normal traffic, halving to
293~URLs/s under both evasion axes.

Finally, we gave PhishLens an explainability layer (exact TreeSHAP attribution,
brand-jacking reason codes, and a calibrated \textsc{uncertain} verdict,
implemented in a working tool) and audited it against the drift-stability
scores. 67.15\% of its attribution weight
rests on features independently flagged drift-brittle, and we traced a
real misclassification (a legitimate Wikipedia URL scored 0.9999) to
precisely that reliance: an explanation faithful today carries no
guarantee of remaining faithful as traffic drifts, which is why
explainability and calibrated abstention should be treated as
complementary safeguards, not substitutes for one another.

Concrete next steps, each with working code already in place: a
larger-scale provenance sweep separating genuine domain failure from
lookup noise, true held-out (not joint-trained) cross-source numbers for
the cascade, multi-seed averaging across B1--B7, and a novelty-gate
formulation that closes B7's remaining precision--recall gap entirely.
